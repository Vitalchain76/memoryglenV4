## Tendai Moyo — 1941–2024 · Bulawayo
slug: `tendai-moyo` · tagline: *A Teacher Whose Lessons Outlived the Bell* · features: livestream, voiceNotes · candles: 86

### His Life

Tendai Moyo was born in Bulawayo in 1941, the third of six children of a railway worker and a mother who kept a small vegetable garden behind the township house. He grew up in Makokoba, where the streets taught him early that a man's word and his work were the same thing. From boyhood he was the child who read under the paraffin lamp long after the house had gone quiet.

He trained as a teacher in the early 1960s and took his first post at a primary school in Mpopoma, where he taught English and arithmetic for nearly a decade. Former pupils still remember how he stayed after hours to help the slowest readers, and how he bought exercise books from his own pay for children whose homes had none.

In 1966 he married Siphiwe Tshuma, a seamstress from Luveve whose laughter, he used to say, could settle any argument in the house. Together they raised four children — Nqobizitha, Thokozile, Mthulisi and Busani — in a home where shoes were polished on Saturday nights and church on Sunday morning was not a matter for negotiation.

In 1978 he was appointed headmaster of a primary school in Luveve, a position he held with humility for more than twenty years. Colleagues called him the headmaster who swept his own office, because he arrived before the cleaners and left after the last child had gone home. Under his care the school sent hundreds of children on to secondary education.

His faith was the quiet engine of his life. An elder in his Methodist congregation for over three decades, he led the men's fellowship, sang bass in the choir, and kept a small red Bible whose pages were soft from use. When neighbours quarrelled or funerals needed organising, it was Baba Moyo the street sent for first.

Like many Zimbabwean families, his children scattered in search of work. Nqobizitha settled in Leeds, England, and Busani in Johannesburg, while Thokozile and Mthulisi remained in Bulawayo. Every Sunday evening, without fail, he sat by the telephone waiting for the calls from abroad, and he kept every grandchild's photograph in a shoebox he called his treasure chest.

He retired in 2001, but retirement only moved his classroom to the veranda, where he drilled grandchildren in their times tables and told them stories of old Bulawayo. Twelve grandchildren and three great-grandchildren grew up on the rhythm of his voice. In his last years he recorded short voice messages of blessing for each of them, one by one.

In his final years his strength faded gently, and in 2024, surrounded by Siphiwe and their children, he fell asleep in the Lord at the age of eighty-three. He had lived long enough to bury old friends and to bless new babies, and he met his end as he had lived — with a hymn on his lips and no bitterness in his heart.

He leaves behind his wife Siphiwe, four children, twelve grandchildren and three great-grandchildren, and a city full of former pupils who measure their own lives against his example. The family requests that those who remember him honour him simply: teach a child something true, and tell them who taught you.

## Sipho Nkosi — 1960–2025 · Durban
slug: `sipho-nkosi` · tagline: *A Harbour Man Who Lifted Everyone Around Him* · features: voiceNotes · candles: 132

### His Life

Sipho Nkosi was born in 1960 in KwaMashu, Durban, the eldest of five children of a cane cutter from the North Coast and a mother whose hymns woke the house before sunrise. As a boy he walked the long road to school barefoot and unbothered, carrying his one exercise book in a plastic bag against the rain. His teachers remembered a tall, laughing child who shared his lunch without being asked.

At nineteen he found work at the Durban harbour, sweeping the sheds before he was trusted with the machines. Over thirty-five years on the docks he rose from general worker to crane operator, lifting containers high above the ships with a steadiness his mates swore by. He never missed a shift in all those years, and he never let a younger man climb where he would not climb himself.

In 1987 he married Busisiwe Mthembu, a nurse's aide from Umlazi, in a wedding the whole street still talks about. They raised three children — Sabelo, Zanele and Ayanda — in a house where the door was never locked to family and the pot on the stove was always bigger than the family needed.

His workmates elected him shop steward, and for fifteen years he carried their grievances to the offices they were afraid to enter. He spoke slowly and listened longer than anyone, and managers learned that when Sipho Nkosi finally stood up to speak, the matter was already as good as settled.

His faith was worn like his overalls — plainly, every day. An elder of his church in Umlazi, he led the men's guild, patched the leaking roof himself, and sat with the families of the dying long after the prayers were done. He said a man who could not sit quietly with another man's grief had not yet learned to pray.

His youngest, Ayanda, settled in England, and the distance pained him more than he ever admitted. He learned the video calls with the patience of a man learning a new machine, and every Sunday after church he held the phone so his grandchildren abroad could see the sea behind him and hear him say, in Zulu, that home was waiting.

He retired in 2020 and took his joy where he had always found it — at the end of a fishing line on the Durban beachfront pier, hat low, thermos at his side. Six grandchildren learned to bait hooks beside him, and every fish, however small, was celebrated like a tournament win.

In the autumn of 2025 he fell ill, and within a short season, surrounded by Busisiwe and their children, he went home to be with the Lord at the age of sixty-five. His last words to his family were a blessing and an instruction: look after your mother, and look after one another.

He leaves his wife Busisiwe, three children, six grandchildren, and a harbour full of men who still tell stories about the steward who listened. The family asks that his memory be honoured in the way he lived — share your lunch, stand up when it matters, and keep the door unlocked.

## Mai Chiweshe — 1938–2023 · Masvingo
slug: `mai-chiweshe` · tagline: *A Mother Whose Table Fed the Whole Street* · features: voiceNotes · candles: 54

### Her Life

Mai Chiweshe was born in 1938 in the Zimuto communal lands outside Masvingo, the first daughter of a farming family that measured its wealth in cattle, rain and children. She learned to plant before she learned to write, and she used to say the soil taught her a patience that no book could ever have given her.

In 1959 she married Takawira Chiweshe, a builder from the neighbouring village, and moved to his homestead, where she bore and raised her children. When four of them grew to adulthood — Sekai, Taurai, Chido and Ropafadzo — she counted herself the richest woman in Zimuto, and she said so often and loudly.

For over forty years she farmed her fields and sold tomatoes, leafy vegetables and her own crochet at Mucheke market. Traders on either side of her stall set their prices by hers, and schoolchildren knew that Mai Chiweshe's stall was where a hungry child could always find a roasted mealie and a scolding delivered with love.

Her hands were never still. In the evenings she sewed school uniforms on a treadle machine by lamplight, mending for widows at no charge and for neighbours at half price. Half the brides of the village walked to their weddings in dresses she had finished the night before, refusing payment beyond a calabash of maize.

Her faith was the foundation under everything she built. A leader in the Ruwadzano women's fellowship, she opened her home for Thursday prayers for thirty years, and her kitchen heard more confessions and consolations than any vestry in Masvingo. She prayed in Shona, sang alto, and believed a hymn could carry a family through any drought.

When her daughter Chido left for England to work as a nurse, Mai Chiweshe wept at the bus rank and then organised the farewell feast. She kept a photograph of every child and grandchild on the wall beside her door, and when visitors asked, she introduced each one — here and abroad — as if presenting royalty.

She buried her husband in 2004 and carried the homestead alone for nearly twenty more years, serving as treasurer of the burial society, arbitrator of disputes, grandmother to eighteen and great-grandmother to nine. In the hard drought years her granary quietly fed households that never spoke of it, because she strictly forbade them to.

In 2023, in her eighty-fifth year, her strength gently ebbed, and she died at home on the homestead with her children around her and hymns at her bedside. She had told them, plainly and without fear, that she was going to see her husband and her Lord, and that they were not to mourn her as people without hope.

She leaves four children, eighteen grandchildren and nine great-grandchildren, spread from Zimuto to London, and a market where her old stall is still called by her name. Her family honours her in the way she taught them: plant in season, feed whoever comes to your gate, and keep singing, even in the drought.

## Thandiwe Dlamini — 1952–2025 · Johannesburg
slug: `thandiwe-dlamini` · tagline: *The Midwife Who Welcomed a Generation* · features: livestream · candles: 201

### Her Life

Thandiwe Dlamini was born in 1952 in Orlando, Soweto, the middle child of a railway clerk and a mother who took in washing. She grew up in a yard shared by four families, where she learned early that care was not a feeling but a practice — water fetched, wounds cleaned, babies rocked while their mothers worked.

In 1974 she qualified as a nurse at Baragwanath, one of the proudest days her family ever photographed. She chose midwifery and stayed with it for thirty-five years, rising to senior midwife, and colleagues estimated that she welcomed more than two thousand babies into the world — a whole generation of Soweto, delivered by her hands.

In 1976 she married Joseph Dlamini, a school sports administrator with a laugh that could fill a stadium, and together they raised three children — Lindiwe, Sipho and Nomvula — in Orlando East. Their home ran on shift schedules and church rotas, yet Sunday lunch was sacred, and no child ever left that table without being heard.

The babies she delivered came back to her as grown men and women, stopping her at the shops to say, Gogo, you caught me. She kept a ledger of every birth for the first decade of her career, and in her last years she could still open it and tell you the weight, the date and the weather.

Her faith was practical and unshaken. President of her parish women's league for twelve years, she organised the choir's robes, the catering for funerals and the quiet visiting of the sick, and she sang alto with a steadiness the whole row leaned on. Prayer, she liked to say, was simply nursing turned toward heaven.

Her eldest, Lindiwe, settled in Toronto as a pharmacist, and Thandiwe carried the separation with the same discipline she had brought to night shifts. She wrote letters long after letters were old-fashioned, recorded voice notes for her Canadian grandchildren, and prayed for each of them by name every single morning of her widowhood.

Joseph died in 2018, and grief did not retire her — it redirected her. From her garage in Orlando East she began a soup kitchen that fed schoolchildren and the elderly three days a week, funded from her pension and the kindness of neighbours. By 2024 it was serving more than a hundred plates a day.

Early in 2025 she fell ill, and the city she had served turned out to serve her — former patients, midwives she had trained, and children from the soup kitchen queuing at her gate with flowers. She died peacefully that autumn, aged seventy-three, with her children at her side and her ledger of births on the bedside table.

She leaves three children, seven grandchildren, and a mourning city that lit more than two hundred candles in her name. Her family asks that she be remembered not with tears alone but with the instruction she gave every new mother she ever served: hold them close, and do not be afraid.

## Kuda Mapfumo — 1947–2021 · Mutare
slug: `kuda-mapfumo` · tagline: *A Quiet Preacher from the Hills of Penhalonga* · features: none · candles: 63

### His Life

Kuda Mapfumo was born in 1947 in Penhalonga, north of Mutare, where the Eastern Highlands rise out of the mist like a wall of green. The son of a subsistence farmer and a potter, he grew up between the fields and the timber plantations, and he always said the hills taught him everything he knew about standing firm.

At eighteen he took work on a timber estate in the Honde Valley, and over thirty years he rose from plantation hand to sawmill foreman. The men under him called him Sekuru even in his forties, because he settled disputes before they ever reached the manager's office and never let a worker be sent home hungry.

In 1974 he married Ratidzo Chikore, a teacher at the local primary school, and they built their home together on the family homestead at Penhalonga. They raised three children — Munyaradzi, Nyasha and Farai — in a household where the Bible and the maize field were read with equal care, season after season.

In his fifties he was ordained a lay preacher in his congregation at Dangamvura, and for twenty years he preached in Shona under trees and in tin-roofed halls across the district. He was not a loud preacher; people said he spoke the way the hills look — quietly — and you found yourself believing.

He chaired the district funeral society for many years, that unsung office which carries every family through its worst day. He kept the register, collected the dues, dug where digging was needed, and no burial in his ward ever failed for want of a man to organise it while the family wept.

His eldest, Munyaradzi, settled in Adelaide, Australia, and Kuda bore the distance with a farmer's patience. He learned to send voice recordings across the world, greeting his Australian grandchildren in Shona and English, and he planted a muhacha tree for each of them, so that something in Zimbabwe was always growing in their name.

He retired from the sawmill in 2007 and gave his last working years to the homestead — bee hives on the slope, a vegetable garden he boasted about, and long afternoons teaching Farai's boys to read the weather. Six grandchildren and two great-grandchildren knew his lap as the safest seat in the Eastern Highlands.

In the quiet winter of 2021 he took ill, and within weeks, with Ratidzo holding his hand and the family singing softly at his bedside, he passed on at the age of seventy-four. Every December the family made the long drive north, and every December the old village watched Sisi Lindi arrive like a festival, her boot full of gifts and her arms full of children.

Her faith community was her second family. Lindiwe sang alto in the church choir for over twenty years, led the women's prayer group, and ran a savings club that quietly carried dozens of families through funerals, fees and emergencies. Grandmotherhood was her final joy. Lindiwe leaves her husband Bhekani, her children Nomonde, Ayanda and Siyabonga, her grandchildren Lwandle and Amahle, her sisters in Kezi, and a great scattered family — from Cape Town to Matabeleland — held together by her decades of remittances, recipes and prayers. A nurse, a mother, a chorister and a bridge between two countries, she spent her life carrying others home. At last, home carried her.

## Chipo Marufu — 1951–2025 · Chegutu

### Her Life

Chipo Marufu was born in 1951 in the Chegutu district, in a year of failed rains when every family was counting its losses. Her arrival was counted a gain instead, and so they named her Chipo — a gift. She grew up grinding maize before school, drawing water at the river, and laughing, her mother said, more than any child had a right to. The name proved prophetic; she spent her whole life giving.

Formal schooling ended early, as it did for many girls of her generation, but Chipo's education never stopped. She could price a basket of tomatoes in her head faster than any till, and she never forgot a face, a debt or a kindness. Her arithmetic was legendary at the market; her memory for people was better. She used to say that school gives you letters, but life gives you sense.

In 1970 she married Tendai Marufu, a railway worker with a bicycle and serious intentions, and moved with him into Chegutu town. They began with one room and one pot, and Chipo started selling vegetables from a basket carried on her head. Within a few years the basket had become a stall, and the stall had become her second home. Tendai used to joke that he married a wife and acquired an institution.

For more than four decades Chipo traded at the Chegutu market — tomatoes, onions, leafy greens, and the dried matemba fish she was famous for. Her scales were honest, her tomatoes were the reddest, and her credit book was an act of faith: mothers in difficulty were told simply to pay when the rains came. More than one family in Chegutu survived a hard season on Chipo's patience and Chipo's tomatoes.

Chipo and Tendai raised three children — Sekai, Munyaradzi and Nyasha — entirely on the proceeds of that market stall. School fees were paid in small notes counted out at the kitchen table, and no child of hers ever missed a term. She named her youngest Nyasha, grace, and friends say she spent the rest of her life illustrating the word. The children grew up knowing that education was their mother's real stall.

Her second calling was music. Chipo joined the church choir as a young wife and led it for over twenty years, her voice rising above the congregation like a lamp. She sang at weddings, at funerals, at dawn services in the cold, teaching the old Shona hymns to generation after generation. She believed firmly that singing was praying twice, and under her hand forty voices learned to become one.

The women's fellowship was her other pulpit. In her uniform and headwrap she organised, cooked, comforted and fundraised, and she carried the fellowship's burdens as naturally as her market basket. She quietly paid exam fees for orphans, visited the sick with pots of soup, and took in a nephew and two nieces when their own homes broke. Nobody kept count of the people Chipo raised; the whole town simply knew.

Widowhood came in 2010, when Tendai passed away after a short illness. Chipo mourned deeply and then, as her family tells it, stood up, tied her headwrap, and went back to the market the following Monday. Her stall and her choir carried her grief, and she carried everyone else's. In her grandchildren — Anotida and Tanaka among them — she found her last great project, teaching them to sing before they could properly talk.

In her final years the market women gave her the corner stall by the gate, the place of honour, though by then she sold more advice than tomatoes. When illness came in 2025, she faced it as she had faced widowhood and drought: with song. Chipo passed away at home in Chegutu, aged seventy-four, with her children and grandchildren around her bed, singing the hymns she had taught them.

She leaves her children Sekai, Munyaradzi and Nyasha, her grandchildren Anotida, Tanaka and their cousins, her choir, her fellowship, and a market that will never sound quite the same. They named her Gift in a year of hunger, and she spent seventy-four years proving her mother right. Chegutu has buried many of its daughters, but few who fed so many, sang so loudly, or gave so completely.

## Themba Sibanda — 1940–2025 · Victoria Falls

### His Life

Themba Sibanda was born in 1940 in a village above the Zambezi, close enough to the great Falls that the spray hung on the horizon like a permanent cloud and the thunder never quite left the air. His people called the Falls Mosi-oa-Tunya — the Smoke That Thunders — and the boy grew up believing, as his father taught him, that the river was a living thing to be respected, read and, when possible, sung to.

His father was a fisherman and tracker, and Themba's classroom was the bush. He learned the moods of hippo, the paths of elephant, and the language of birds long before he learned English. He herded cattle, paddled dugouts, and sat through evenings of elders' stories until the stars wheeled overhead. Everything he later became — guide, teacher, storyteller — was already present in that watchful, hopeful boy beside the river.

They had named him Themba — hope — and in the hard years of his youth the name became his discipline. In the early 1960s he took work as a boat hand and porter at one of the first safari camps near the Falls, carrying bags and listening. He learned his English from visitors, his patience from the river, and his trade from the old trackers. Within a few seasons he was guiding trips of his own.

For over forty years Themba guided on and around the Zambezi — river trips at sunset, walks through the rainforest at the Falls, safaris into the game country beyond. He could read water the way scholars read scripture, and guests remembered not just the elephants and rainbows he showed them but the stories he told while showing them. Through war, change and independence, he kept guiding, and kept hoping, as his name required.

In 1965 he married Ntombizodwa, a teacher's daughter with a laugh he said was worth more than any tip a tourist ever gave him. They built their home in the township near the Falls and raised three children — Zenzele, Sipho and Duduzile — on guide's wages and a mother's fierce discipline. Themba worked the river by day and told its stories at home by night; the children grew up half-wild, well-schooled and deeply loved.

As the decades passed he became the guide other guides consulted. He trained scores of young men from the township in tracking, boatcraft and the old knowledge, insisting that the river's dangers demanded humility before anything else. His evening storytelling gatherings at the homestead became an institution; neighbours' children arrived for the stories and stayed for the proverbs. Many a guide working at the Falls today began on Themba's veranda.

His faith anchored everything. Themba served as a deacon in his church, sang the deep bass line of the Ndebele hymns, and prayed before every river trip, whatever the guests believed. A guide who does not pray, he liked to say, is just a man in a boat. His thanksgiving after safe journeys, his generosity at the church door, and his open home made him as much pastor as guide.

Retirement, when it finally came, suited him. He carved wooden animals for visitors from his chair under the msasa tree, walked with his stick to watch the spray rise over the rainforest, and presided over a growing tribe of grandchildren — Mvelo and Sanelisiwe among the first. With Duduzile settled across the border in Botswana and former clients writing from London and beyond, his veranda became a small parliament of the world's affection.

In 2025, in his eighty-fifth year, Themba passed away peacefully at home, with Ntombizodwa beside him and the distant thunder of the Falls, his family swear, unusually loud that week. Tributes arrived from across the world — from tourists he had guided decades before, from guides he had trained, from a river community that had known him simply as Sekulu, grandfather. The old guide had completed his longest journey.

Themba leaves his beloved wife Ntombizodwa, his children Zenzele, Sipho and Duduzile, his grandchildren Mvelo, Sanelisiwe and their cousins, and a fraternity of guides who carry his knowledge on the water every single day. Named Hope in 1940, he lived up to his name through every season of a changing country. The river runs on, the spray still rises, and those who knew him hear his stories in the thunder.
